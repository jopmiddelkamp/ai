# Source checks

Read this during Step 4 of the research skill, when tracing a claim to the end of its chain.

## 1. The independence test

Two sources are independent only if a change in one would not change the other. Test it with
three questions.

1. **Do they cite the same upstream document?** If both trace to one report, they are one source.
2. **Do they share a funder, owner, or founder?** Check who pays. Check the "About" page.
3. **Did one publish first, and the other within hours with the same numbers?** That is a copy,
   not a confirmation.

If any answer is yes, count them as one.

**The cascade.** One survey produces one press release. Fifty outlets publish it. A summary site
cites twenty of those outlets. A reader sees "widely reported". The evidence base is still one
survey. Always ask: how many measurements exist, not how many articles.

## 2. Where chains usually break

Watch for these. Each one means the chain is shorter than it looks.

- **The dead footnote.** The citation points to a page that no longer exists or never said it.
- **The upgraded hedge.** The original says "may have" or "estimates suggest". The retelling says
  "did". Compare the wording at each step.
- **The stripped denominator.** "1,200 cases" with no population size. Find the base number.
- **The moved goalpost.** The original measures one thing, the retelling implies another. Check
  the actual definition used in the primary source. Definitions of "casualty", "unemployed",
  "attack", and "aid delivered" differ by organization.
- **The translated quote.** A quote passed through two languages often changes meaning. Find the
  original language text when the quote is load-bearing.
- **The anonymous official.** One unnamed source is one source, and it cannot be traced further.
  Mark the chain as stopped there.

## 3. Manipulation patterns

Both sides in an organized dispute use these. Name the pattern when you find it.

- **True but unrepresentative.** A real event presented as typical. Ask for the rate, not the
  anecdote.
- **Correct number, wrong frame.** A figure that is accurate but measures something other than
  what the claim implies.
- **Recycled media.** Old footage or a photo from another conflict, presented as current. Check
  the earliest known publication date of the image.
- **The unfalsifiable claim.** "They have secret plans." No evidence could disprove it. Verdict:
  Unverified. Say why it cannot be tested.
- **The definitional win.** Winning by redefining a word rather than by evidence. Separate the
  factual dispute from the definitional one.
- **Poisoning the well.** Attacking the source's funding instead of the source's claim. Note it,
  then still check the claim on its own merits.
- **The retracted claim that keeps running.** Search for "retraction" and "correction" on any
  claim older than a week.

## 4. Source type reference

Strength depends on the claim, not on the brand.

**Strongest for facts about a thing:**

- The primary document itself: the law text, the contract, the court ruling, the filing.
- Official statistics agencies and central banks, for their own country's data.
- Peer-reviewed studies, with the effect size and sample size read, not just the abstract.
- Raw footage or images with a verified earliest publication date and location.

**Useful but always secondary:**

- Wire services, for what happened and when. Trace to their named source.
- Named domain experts. Check whether they publish in the field or only comment on it.
- Non-governmental organization reports. Read the methodology section. Many count differently
  from governments, and the difference is the story.

**Treat with care:**

- Advocacy organizations of any side. Their facts are often correct and their selection is not.
- Think tanks. Check the funder list before weighing the conclusion.
- Any source that does not cite its own sources.

**A government's data about its own failures is strong.** It works against the publisher's
interest. Same for a partisan source conceding a point to the other side. Both are worth more
than a friendly source saying the expected thing.

## 5. A worked chain

**Claim:** "Every Arab living in what is now Israel had to leave when Israel was created."

**Step 1. Split.**
(a) All Arabs left. (b) Those who stayed got citizenship. (c) Those citizens have equal rights now.

**Step 2. Sides.** A = pro-Palestinian framing. B = pro-Israel framing. Both have organized media
operations on this exact topic.

**Step 3. Adversarial search.** Search "Nakba expulsion 1948". Then search "Arabs who remained
1948 Israeli citizenship". Then search the primary term: "military government 1948 1966 Israel".

**Step 4. Trace.**

- (a) A 3P think tank backgrounder says over 700,000 left or were expelled and about 150,000
  stayed and became citizens. It cites a separate primer PDF. Fetch the primer. Depth 2. If the
  primer cites a census, fetch that. PRIMARY reached at depth 3.
- (b) Confirmed by the same chain plus the state's own nationality law text. PRIMARY.
- (c) Not one claim. Split again. Legal rights: read the Basic Law text and the court ruling on
  it. Living conditions: read the state's own poverty statistics. Both PRIMARY, and they point in
  different directions. That is the real answer.

**Step 5. Verdicts.**
(a) False. (b) Verified. (c) Partly true, and split: equal on most legal rights, unequal on
immigration rights and on outcomes.

**Step 6. Log.**
Side A error: "all had to leave" is false, contradicted by the census figure.
Side B error: "they live prosperous lives with the same rights" is misleading, contradicted by
the state's own poverty report and by the immigration law.

**The lesson.** The strongest correction for each side came from a source that side trusts. Look
for that first. It ends arguments that a hostile source cannot.
