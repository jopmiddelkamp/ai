## Q1: "I have a great product but nobody's buying it. What's wrong?"

**Answer:**
This is almost always one of 4 problems (not 1):

**Problem A: MESSAGING** (most common)
You understand your product but your customer doesn't.
Fix: Apply StoryBrand. Your website should say in 5 seconds:
"Who is this for → What problem does it solve → What do they do next."
If it says "We are the leading provider of..." — that's your problem.

**Problem B: OFFER** (second most common)
The product is good but the OFFER is weak.
An offer = product + price + terms + guarantee + bonuses.
Fix: Apply Hormozi. Score your offer on all 4 Value Equation levers.
If you don't have a guarantee, add one. Immediately.

**Problem C: WRONG AUDIENCE**
Good product, wrong people seeing it.
Fix: Get specific on ICP (Ideal Customer Profile).
One specific person with one specific problem.
Go find exactly those people. Don't wait for them to find you.

**Problem D: TRUST**
They need it, they understand it, but they don't trust you enough to buy.
Fix: Build social proof. Case studies, testimonials, results.
"People like them" getting specific results.

**Diagnostic test:** Ask 5 people who said no WHY they said no.
Don't defend. Just listen. The pattern in their answers tells you which problem you have.

## Q2: "How do I know when to hire my first employee?"

**Answer:**
Three-part test from Buy Back Your Time + E-Myth:

**Part 1: The Buyback Rate Test**
Calculate your target hourly rate: annual income goal ÷ 2000 hours.
If you're regularly spending more than 20% of your time on tasks
below that rate: time to hire someone who does those tasks full-time.

**Part 2: The Bottleneck Test**
Is there revenue you're leaving on the table because you don't have
capacity? If yes: hiring creates ROI immediately. Hire now.

**Part 3: The Documentation Test**
Can you hand this job off with a written process + a week of training?
If no: document the job first. Then hire.

**What to hire for first:**
Almost always: administrative/operational support before sales or technical.
Why: Frees up YOUR highest-value time first.
Counter-intuitive but consistently correct.

**The hiring rule:** Hire slower than feels comfortable. Fire faster than feels comfortable.

## Q3: "My team doesn't hold each other accountable. How do I fix it?"

**Answer:**
This is Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions — specifically the Level 4 problem (Avoidance of Accountability) which exists because of Level 1 (Absence of Trust) or Level 3 (Lack of Commitment).

**Fix the root, not the symptom:**

If people don't commit to decisions (Level 3 first):
→ In every meeting: make the decision explicit and stated
→ "So we've decided: X will happen by Y. Everyone clear?"
→ Write it down in real time. Review next week.

If there's no trust (Level 1 first):
→ Run a vulnerability exercise: each person shares one professional failure
→ Leader goes first. Every time.
→ Gradually build the muscle of honesty

**The peer accountability solution:**
Don't wait for the leader to hold people accountable.
Build it into the meeting structure.
Weekly scorecard review: each number is owned by one person.
If it's red: they explain why. Team asks questions.
Not punishment — information.

**The structural fix (from EOS):**
Weekly L10 meeting with scorecard. Every number. Every week.
Red numbers go to issues list. Issues get solved.
Accountability is built into the system, not the culture.

## Q4: "I'm trying to decide between two strategic directions. How do I choose?"

**Answer:**
Use three frameworks simultaneously:

**Framework 1: Playing to Win (Roger Martin)**
For each option, ask: Does this represent a real choice about
WHERE to play and HOW to win? Or is it just doing more of the same?
A strategy that doesn't narrow your focus isn't a strategy.
Which option forces you to say NO to more things? That's usually the real strategy.

**Framework 2: The Hedgehog (Collins)**
Which option sits at the intersection of:
- What you're passionate about
- What you could be best in the world at
- What drives your economic engine?
If one option scores higher on all three, that's your answer.

**Framework 3: The Regret Minimization Framework (Bezos)**
Imagine you're 80 years old. Looking back.
Which choice, if you hadn't made it, would you regret more?
Often cuts through the logical noise.

**Then: Apply the 5-Move Thinking (Bet-David)**
Play out 5 moves ahead for EACH option.
What does the world look like if you choose A vs. B in 2 years? 5 years?
Which path creates more options (not fewer)?

**The meta-answer:** You probably already know. Which did you hope was right before you started analyzing?

## Q5: "How do I build a culture that doesn't depend on me being in the room?"

**Answer:**
Culture Code (Coyle) + No Rules Rules (Hastings) + Good to Great (Collins):

**The 3-layer culture building system:**

**Layer 1: Define it (takes 1 hour)**
Core values = the 3-5 behaviors you hire and fire by.
Not aspirational. Real.
"If someone violated this value, we would let them go even if their performance numbers were good."
Test each value with that question. Keep only the ones that pass.

**Layer 2: Model it (takes constant intention)**
Culture is not what you say. It's what you do when things are hard.
Do you fire high performers who violate values? (Yes = culture is real)
Do you keep them? (Yes = culture is aspirational wallpaper)

Leader behaviors that create culture:
→ Admit mistakes publicly ("I got that wrong")
→ Credit others publicly ("That was Sarah's idea")
→ Ask questions instead of giving answers in meetings
→ Thank people for raising problems (not punishing them)

**Layer 3: Systematize it**
Hiring: Interview for values (not just skills)
    Ask: "Tell me about a time you had to make a values-based decision."
Onboarding: Tell your culture STORIES (not values statements)
    "Let me tell you about a time we lived [value]..."
Performance: Review against values + metrics (not just metrics)
Reviews: Grade people on how they embody values (score it 1-5)

**The Hastings insight:** The best culture document is not a list of values.
It's a description of how you behave when things are hard.

## Q6: "I need to raise prices but I'm scared of losing clients. What do I do?"

**Answer:**
This is almost always a false fear. Here's the data and the process:

**The data:**
Most businesses that raise prices lose 10-20% of clients.
But if your price increase is 25-30%: your revenue goes UP even with client loss.
And the clients you lose are almost always the highest-maintenance, lowest-margin ones.

**The Hormozi framework:**
Your price sends a signal. Low price = low quality in the buyer's mind.
Premium price attracts premium clients who:
→ Pay on time
→ Refer others
→ Don't complain
→ Value what you deliver

Low prices attract clients who don't value you.

**The process for raising prices:**
Step 1: Announce to existing clients with 60-90 days notice
Step 2: Frame it as investment in better service
"We're increasing our pricing to allow us to continue investing in [specific improvements]."
Step 3: Honor existing rates for best clients (if desired) for 6-12 months
Step 4: Apply new pricing to all new clients immediately
Step 5: Track client retention and revenue. You'll be surprised.

**The conversation:**
"As of [date], our pricing will be [new price].
This allows us to [specific investment/improvement].
I wanted to make sure you heard directly from me."

Don't apologize. Don't negotiate. State it as fact.

## Q7: "My salesperson isn't hitting their number. What do I do?"

**Answer:**
Diagnose before acting. There are 4 different problems that look identical:

**Problem 1: Activity problem (they're not doing enough)**
They don't have enough calls, meetings, or proposals.
Fix: Fix the inputs. Set activity minimums. Track daily.
"X calls, Y meetings, Z proposals per week — non-negotiable."

**Problem 2: Skill problem (they can't convert)**
They're active but conversion rate is below benchmark.
Fix: Call recordings, ride-alongs, role-play, training.
Which step of the sales process is the bottleneck?
(Prospecting → Qualifying → Discovery → Demo → Proposal → Close)

**Problem 3: Motivation problem (they've given up)**
They're going through the motions but checked out.
Fix: Direct conversation. What's wrong? What do they need?
Sometimes it's the wrong role. Sometimes it's fixable.
Use Radical Candor. Be direct. Be caring.

**Problem 4: Wrong person (they can't do this job)**
No amount of coaching or incentive will fix it.
Fix: Make the call. The longer you wait, the more it costs you.
(Costs: their salary, missed revenue, team morale, your time)

**The decision framework:**
Give someone 90 days of focused coaching.
If no improvement: they're not going to improve.
Make the call.

## Q8: "I have too many ideas and can't focus. How do I prioritize?"

**Answer:**
The One Thing (Keller) + EOS Rocks + Your Next 5 Moves (Bet-David):

**Step 1: The Great Purge**
Write EVERY idea on separate cards/sticky notes.
Get them all out of your head.
Now they're not competing for mental space.

**Step 2: The Focusing Question**
For each idea: "What's the ONE thing I can do with this idea such that
by doing it, everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?"

**Step 3: The Filter**
Apply to every idea:
→ Does this move me toward my 10-year vision?
→ Can I accomplish meaningful progress this quarter?
→ Is this an entrepreneurial seizure (exciting but distracting)?

Honest answer usually kills 70% of ideas.

**Step 4: The Quarterly Rocks**
From what remains: What are the 3-5 things that, if done THIS QUARTER,
most advance the vision?
Those become Rocks. Everything else waits.

**The rule:** Nothing can be a priority if everything is a priority.
Your job is to choose. The cost of choosing wrong is lower than the cost of not choosing.

**The CEO's calendar as prioritization:**
Block your first 4 hours every morning for your #1 priority.
Let everything else compete for the remaining time.
If it doesn't get scheduled, it wasn't actually a priority.

## Q9: "Should I raise money or bootstrap?"

**Answer:**
Zero to One (Thiel) + The Founder's Dilemmas (Wasserman) + Main Street Millionaire (Sanchez):

**The frameworks:**

**Bootstrapping wins when:**
→ Your business can be cash-flow positive within 12 months
→ Your market doesn't reward winner-take-all speed
→ You want to maintain control and flexibility
→ Your margins are high (services, software, content)
→ You're building something you want to OWN, not sell

**Raising wins when:**
→ Speed of market capture determines winner (network effects)
→ Capital requirements are unavoidable (manufacturing, deep tech)
→ You have a clear path to large outcome (VC math: 100x return)
→ You want to move fast and don't mind dilution
→ You have relationships with good investors (smart money matters)

**The founder's dilemma:**
Rich vs. King. You rarely get both.
Raise money: Faster growth, less control, potential for bigger outcome.
Bootstrap: Slower growth, full control, outcome depends on profits.

**The question that cuts through:**
"Would I be proud to own 100% of a $3M business, or do I want a
shot at 15% of a $100M business?" 
Different goals. Neither is wrong.

**For Main Street businesses (Sanchez):**
Almost never raise VC. Seller financing + SBA is almost always better.
VC math doesn't work for businesses that aren't software.

## Q10: "How do I get my first 10 customers?"

**Answer:**
Never Eat Alone (Ferrazzi) + The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick) + $100M Leads (Hormozi):

**The rule for first 10: Unscalable is fine.**
Don't try to build systems. Go get them manually.
Amazon's first sales were Bezos individually mailing books.
LinkedIn's first users were invited one by one.
Airbnb founders stayed in their own listings to meet early guests.

**The process:**

**Step 1: List 50 people who might need what you offer**
Not strangers. People you know or know of.
Former colleagues, industry contacts, friends of friends.

**Step 2: Have 1:1 conversations (not pitches)**
Mom Test questions: "Are you dealing with [problem]?"
If yes: "Would you be open to trying something new?"
If yes: "I'm building [thing]. Would you be our first client?"

**Step 3: Offer a pilot pricing**
"As a founding member, you get [price] instead of [full price]."
Or: "Free for first 30 days if you give us detailed feedback."

**Step 4: Deliver exceptionally**
Your first 10 customers are your salesforce.
They will either refer 3 more each or warn others.
Over-deliver. Every time.

**Step 5: Ask for referrals**
At peak happiness: "Who else do you know who has this problem?"
The best time to ask: Right after they experience their first win.

**The Ferrazzi rule:** Give before you take.
Help them with something before asking them to be a customer.

## Q11: "My competitors are copying me. What do I do?"

**Answer:**
Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim/Mauborgne) + Zero to One (Thiel) + Good to Great (Collins):

**Reframe first:**
Being copied means you found something that works. That's good news.
The question is: How do you stay ahead?

**The 3 protection strategies:**

**Strategy 1: Move to Blue Ocean (escape competition)**
Stop playing on the same field.
ERRC framework: What can you Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create?
If they copy your current offer: make your current offer irrelevant.

**Strategy 2: Compound your advantages**
From Good to Great: Great companies build flywheels.
Every advantage feeds the next.
By the time competitors copy Step 1: You're on Step 5.
The answer to copying is speed, not secrecy.

**Strategy 3: Build switching costs**
Make it expensive (in time, money, relationships) for customers to leave.
Deep integrations, proprietary data, relationships, customization.
A competitor can copy your features. They can't copy your customer relationships.

**The Thiel question:**
"What are you doing that can't be copied?"
If the answer is "nothing" — that's your strategy problem.
1 to n is always copable. 0 to 1 is not.
Find the 0 to 1 in your business. Double down there.

## Q12: "My team is burned out. What do I do?"

**Answer:**
The Culture Code (Coyle) + Extreme Ownership (Willink) + The 5 Types of Wealth (Bloom):

**Diagnosis first:**
Burnout has different causes:

**Cause 1: Too much work, not enough people**
Fix: Hire. Delegate. Prioritize ruthlessly.
"Not everything is urgent. Let's agree on what actually is."

**Cause 2: Work without meaning or context**
Fix: Connect daily work to mission constantly.
"Here's why what you're doing matters."
Tell stories of impact. Often.

**Cause 3: Lack of autonomy**
Fix: Give people ownership, not just tasks.
"This is yours. I trust you. Here's the decision-making authority you have."

**Cause 4: Bad manager**
Fix: The most common reason good people burn out and quit.
They don't leave companies. They leave managers.
Address the manager. Don't let it fester.

**Cause 5: Culture of perpetual emergency**
Fix: Some urgency is healthy. Constant urgency is toxic.
"We've been in crisis mode for 6 months. Let's build systems so we're not."

**The Extreme Ownership response:**
"My team is burned out" = "I created conditions that burned them out."
What can I change? What did I not do that I should have?

**Immediate interventions:**
→ Cancel 20% of their lowest-value commitments
→ Add Friday afternoon protection (no meetings, focus work only)
→ Publicly recognize their sacrifice with specific examples
→ Ask each person: "What can I take off your plate this week?"

## Q13: "How do I build a business that runs without me?"

**Answer:**
E-Myth (Gerber) + Buy Back Your Time (Martell) + Traction (Wickman):

**The 4-level business maturity model:**

**Level 1: SELF-EMPLOYED**
You = the business. Business stops when you stop.
This is where most people get stuck and call it entrepreneurship.

**Level 2: OPERATOR**
You have a team, but they depend on you for decisions.
You've grown, but you haven't freed yourself.

**Level 3: MANAGER**
You manage managers who manage the work.
You're needed strategically, not operationally.

**Level 4: OWNER**
The business runs on systems and people.
Your involvement is optional, not required.

**The path from Level 1 to Level 4:**

STEP 1: Document everything you do
Every repeatable task: write the process.
Target: Someone with no context could follow the document.

STEP 2: Hire to replace yourself at each level
Start with $10/hour work (admin).
End with $500/hour work (strategy).
Each hire frees you to focus higher.

STEP 3: Install your OS
EOS/Traction gives you a system that runs the company.
L10 meetings, Rocks, Scorecard = the company runs on rhythm not heroism.

STEP 4: Build your leadership team
They need to be able to run the business for 2 weeks without you.
Then 2 months. Then indefinitely.

STEP 5: Redefine your role
Founder → CEO → Investor → Advisor
Where do you want to end up?

**The timeline:**
Realistic: 2-5 years to fully remove yourself from operations.
Rush it: Takes longer (systems collapse without foundation).
Build it right: The business that runs without you is worth 3-5x more when you sell.

## Q14: "What's the best way to price my services?"

**Answer:**
$100M Offers (Hormozi) + Playing to Win (Martin) + Never Split the Difference (Voss):

**The 3 pricing philosophies:**

**COST-PLUS (worst)**
Calculate your costs + add margin.
Problem: Ignores value delivered. Almost always underprices.

**MARKET-RATE (mediocre)**
Look at competitors. Price similarly.
Problem: Assumes you're the same. Prevents differentiation.

**VALUE-BASED (best)**
Price based on the value the client receives.
"If this generates $500K for you, what's 10% of that worth?"

**The value-based calculation:**
1. Quantify the outcome in dollars (revenue gained OR cost saved)
2. Apply a "fraction of value" logic (charge 10-25% of value created)
3. That's your floor. Price confidence above it.

**The Hormozi anchoring sequence:**
State their problem cost: "Most companies lose $X to this problem annually."
Describe alternatives: "An in-house team would cost $X. Consultants charge $X."
State your price: "[Your price] — and here's what you get."
Add guarantee: "And if [result] doesn't happen, [consequence]."

**The Voss negotiation:**
Don't justify your price. State it with confidence. Then silence.
If they push: "What makes you say that?"
If they push more: "I understand. Help me understand what you're comparing this to."
If they push more: Calibrated question. "How would this work at that number?"
Never: Immediately discount. It signals your price was wrong.

**Raising prices:**
Raise by 20-30% every year until you hit resistance.
When you start losing more than 20% of prospects on price: you've found your market ceiling.

## Q15: "I'm thinking about acquiring a business. How do I evaluate it?"

**Answer:**
Main Street Millionaire (Sanchez) + Principles (Dalio) + Never Split the Difference (Voss):

**THE DUE DILIGENCE FRAMEWORK:**

**FINANCIAL VALIDATION (most important):**
1. Get 3 years of TAX RETURNS (not P&L — people lie in P&L, not tax returns)
2. Reconcile bank statements to tax returns month by month
3. Calculate TRUE SDE (see calculation in frameworks file)
4. Verify the multiple makes sense for the industry
5. Identify any one-time revenue that won't recur
6. Check accounts receivable aging (old receivables are red flags)

**OPERATIONAL VALIDATION:**
1. Understand every employee and their role
2. Identify: Who would leave if owner leaves?
3. Customer concentration: No client > 20-25% of revenue
4. Contract review: Are revenue contracts transferable?
5. Vendor contracts: Will they honor them for new owner?

**MARKET VALIDATION:**
1. Is this industry growing, stable, or declining?
2. Who are the competitors and how are they doing?
3. What is the business's competitive advantage?
4. Is the advantage tied to the owner or the business?

**THE NEGOTIATION:**
Use calibrated questions: "Help me understand how you arrived at this price."
Use labeling: "It seems like you're very emotionally attached to this number."
Use anchoring: Offer 65% of asking, explain with data.
Use seller financing: Always ask for it.

**THE DECISION:**
Go if: SDE multiple is fair, no concentration risk, systems exist, team is solid
No-go if: Revenue tied to owner personality, one dominant customer, books don't reconcile

## Q16: "How do I get better at making decisions quickly?"

**Answer:**
Principles (Dalio) + Think Again (Grant) + Your Next Five Moves (Bet-David):

**DALIO'S DECISION-MAKING SYSTEM:**

Step 1: What type of decision is this?
- Reversible vs. irreversible? (reversible: decide fast, learn)
- High stakes vs. low stakes? (high stakes: slow down)
- Within my circle of competence? (outside: get help)

Step 2: What are the base rates?
"When a company of this type, at this stage, in this market,
has made this decision — what typically happened?"
Base rates are more reliable than your unique insight.

Step 3: Stress test with a "pre-mortem"
"Assume this decision fails completely. Why did it fail?"
Working backwards from failure reveals hidden risks.

Step 4: Find your "believable people"
Who has relevant experience and good judgment on this?
Weight their input heavily. Discount motivated reasoning.

**THE DECISION TYPES (from Bezos):**
Type 1: Irreversible, consequential → Go slow, be thorough
Type 2: Reversible, low stakes → Go fast, learn from doing

Most decisions are Type 2. Most people treat them like Type 1.
This is why most people are slow AND don't learn as fast.

**THE THINK AGAIN ADDITION:**
Before finalizing: "What would change my mind?"
If nothing comes to mind: You're being a preacher, not a scientist.
Find one piece of evidence that would make you choose differently.
Sit with that evidence before deciding.

**THE BET-DAVID SYSTEM:**
For any big decision: Map 5 moves ahead.
"If I choose A, what happens? Then what? Then what?"
"If I choose B, what happens? Then what? Then what?"
The decision tree usually makes the answer obvious.

## Q17: "I want to build a personal brand. Where do I start?"

**Answer:**
Building a StoryBrand (Miller) + Contagious (Berger) + The Almanack of Naval Ravikant:

**NAVAL'S FOUNDATION:**
Personal brand = specific knowledge + unique voice + public sharing.

Specific knowledge: What do you know that almost nobody else knows?
(Not textbook knowledge — knowledge from your unique experience)
Unique voice: How you see the world differently.
Public sharing: Making it visible consistently.

**THE POSITIONING QUESTION:**
"Who specifically do I help, with what specifically, to achieve what specifically?"
Until you can answer in one sentence, you don't have a brand.
You have a hobby.

**THE CONTENT SYSTEM:**
What's your distribution channel? Pick ONE. Master it. Then expand.
(Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube, Twitter/X, Newsletter, Podcast)

What's your content angle? One of these:
→ Teach (share frameworks and knowledge)
→ Document (share your journey in real-time)
→ Inspire (share stories that motivate)
→ Entertain (make it enjoyable to consume)

What's your posting cadence?
More important than quality, at least initially.
Consistency > Excellence for the first 6 months.
You're building the habit and the audience simultaneously.

**THE BERGER/CONTAGIOUS CONTENT FRAMEWORK:**
Does your content have Social Currency? (makes sharer look good)
Is it triggered? (reminds people of you regularly)
Is it emotional? (high arousal: awe, excitement, not low arousal: contentment)
Is it practical? (news they can use, share to help someone)
Does it tell a story? (people remember stories, not facts)

**THE COMPOUNDING INSIGHT (Naval):**
Personal brands compound like interest.
Year 1: Almost nothing happens. Almost everyone quits.
Year 2: Small audience builds.
Year 3: Opportunities come to you.
Year 5+: You can't replicate this with any amount of money.
Plant the tree. The shade comes later.

## Q18: "How do I deal with a difficult competitor?"

**Answer:**
Blue Ocean (Kim) + The Art of War (Sun Tzu) + Influence (Cialdini):

**SUN TZU'S PRINCIPLES (business application):**

"Know your enemy as you know yourself."
Competitive intelligence BEFORE strategy.
→ What are they genuinely good at?
→ What do their customers actually say (reviews, forums)?
→ Where are their weaknesses? (service gaps, pricing, geography)
→ Who is their ideal customer? (this tells you who NOT to chase)

"Attack where they are not."
Don't fight their strengths.
Find the customer they're ignoring. Serve them perfectly.
Find the geography they haven't entered. Dominate it.
Find the offer they can't match (usually the opposite of their positioning).

"Win without fighting."
The best competitive position makes fighting irrelevant.
→ If you're premium: their low price is irrelevant
→ If you're niche: their breadth is irrelevant
→ If you're specialized: their generalism is irrelevant

**THE BLUE OCEAN RESPONSE:**
Stop competing. Create a new category.
"We're not competing with [competitor]. We're building [new thing]."
Category creators don't fight for market share — they define the market.

**THE CIALDINI DEFENSIVE PLAY:**
They're copying your features? Compete on relationship (Liking).
They're price-cutting? Compete on authority (Authority signals).
They're spreading faster? Build community (Unity).
Features can be copied. Relationships cannot.

**The meta-answer:**
The best response to competition is to get so good at serving
your specific customer that price and features become irrelevant.
"We're not the cheapest. We're the best for you."

## Q19: "My revenue is inconsistent. How do I create predictability?"

**Answer:**
Profit First (Michalowicz) + Traction (Wickman) + $100M Leads (Hormozi):

**THE 3 CAUSES OF REVENUE INCONSISTENCY:**

**Cause 1: No recurring revenue model**
Project-based revenue = feast or famine.
Every month: start from zero.
Fix: Convert some or all of your offer to recurring.
Retainers, subscriptions, annual contracts.
Even 30% recurring revenue transforms your business stability.

**Cause 2: Inconsistent pipeline activity**
You sell when you need money. You deliver when you have clients.
You can't do both at the same time.
Fix: Constant pipeline activity regardless of current workload.
This is the most important discipline in service businesses.
EOS: Pipeline activity as a weekly scorecard metric.

**Cause 3: Customer concentration**
One or two clients = a significant percent of revenue.
When they leave (they will), it's a crisis.
Fix: Maximum 20% of revenue from any single client.
Actively diversify before you're forced to.

**THE PREDICTABILITY SYSTEM:**
1. Track your sales pipeline WEEKLY (not monthly)
2. Measure lead-to-close time (how long does your sales cycle take?)
3. Forecast based on pipeline × historical close rate
4. Set pipeline minimum (if pipeline < X, pause everything else, sell)
5. Build recurring revenue to cover fixed costs minimum

**THE CASH BUFFER:**
From Profit First:
Minimum: 2 months of operating expenses in cash
Better: 3-4 months
Best: 6 months

With 6 months cash buffer: You make better decisions.
You're not desperate. You don't discount. You don't take bad clients.

## Q20: "I want to sell my business in 3-5 years. What do I do now?"

**Answer:**
Main Street Millionaire (Sanchez) + Good to Great (Collins) + The E-Myth (Gerber):

**THE EXIT PREPARATION FRAMEWORK:**

**3-5 YEARS OUT:**

Year 1-2: Build systems
→ Document every core process
→ Build the management team that can run it without you
→ Install EOS or similar operating system
→ Goal: Business runs 90 days without you, revenue doesn't drop

Year 2-3: Optimize financials
→ Clean up your books (get on accrual accounting)
→ Maximize SDE (remove personal expenses from business)
→ Build 3 years of clean financial history
→ Reduce customer concentration below 20% per client
→ Goal: Financial story that a buyer can trust

Year 3-5: Build the narrative
→ What makes this business uniquely valuable?
→ What are the growth opportunities a buyer can execute?
→ What makes this business defensible (moat)?
→ Goal: A business with a compelling future, not just a good past

**WHAT INCREASES MULTIPLE:**
→ Recurring revenue (subscriptions, contracts, retainers)
→ High gross margins (60%+ is premium)
→ Low customer concentration (spread risk)
→ Systems and documentation (turnkey)
→ Strong management team (doesn't need founder)
→ Growing market (tailwind)
→ Proprietary technology or process (defensibility)

**WHAT DECREASES MULTIPLE:**
→ Revenue tied to founder relationships
→ One or two clients = majority of revenue
→ No documented processes
→ Key person dependencies in operations
→ Declining market

**THE COLLINS INSIGHT:**
Businesses that sell at premium multiples didn't build for sale.
They built for greatness. Premium buyers pay for greatness.
Build a great business. The exit takes care of itself.

**BROKERS vs. DIRECT:**
