## THE ONE LINE
**Design the product around the price** — not the other way around. Most product failures are not engineering failures, they're pricing failures dressed up as engineering failures.

## THE CORE INSIGHT

```
WRONG ORDER (most companies)        RIGHT ORDER (Ramanujam & Tacke)
─────────────────────────────       ──────────────────────────────────
Design → Build → Market → Price     Market & Price → Design → Build

Pricing decided LAST                 Pricing baked in FIRST
~5% of business cases have real      Real willingness-to-pay validated
WTP data                             with customers before design freezes
"Build it and they'll come"          "Design around what they'll pay"
```

Per Simon-Kucher's own client data, **~72% of new products fail.** The data is self-reported by the consultancy that benefits from hiring around it — directional, not independently verified. The pattern, though, is real: companies skip willingness-to-pay (WTP) discovery and discover their pricing error after launch.

## THE 4 FAILURE TYPES — DIAGNOSE BEFORE FIXING

```
FEATURE SHOCK
  Crammed too many features → confusing, overpriced.
  Cultural cause: engineering-driven product team.
  Tell: can't articulate the value in one sentence; price slashed post-launch.
  Example: Amazon Fire Phone ($170M write-down).

MINIVATION
  Right product, priced TOO LOW.
  Cultural cause: risk-averse leadership.
  Tell: sales easily hits target; sellouts; channel pockets the margin.
  Example: Audi Q7 (missed ~€210M/yr by under-pricing).

HIDDEN GEM
  Great idea, never properly launched.
  Cultural cause: leadership coddles the core business.
  Tell: mid-level execs kill it; sold as a deal sweetener; rival ships first.
  Example: Kodak's digital camera (1974).

UNDEAD
  Wrong answer to the wrong question — or no question asked.
  Cultural cause: top-down pet project, no dissent allowed.
  Tell: sales avoid raising it; "senior management really likes this one."
  Example: Segway, Google Glass.
```

## THE 5 PRICING MYTHS — COUNTER THESE

```
1. "Build it and they will come"             — Hides 95% of failures
2. "Innovation needs isolated artists"        — Customer input INFORMS, doesn't pollute
3. "High failure rates are normal"            — Failure is preventable
4. "Customers need the product to price it"   — False; they react to CONCEPTS
5. "You can't price what you haven't built"   — Cost-plus thinking; price is set by VALUE
```

## THE 9 RULES — SUMMARY

```
 1. WTP TALK EARLY               Have it BEFORE the design freezes
 2. NEEDS-BASED SEGMENTATION     Segment by needs/value/WTP, not demographics
 3. CONFIGURATION & BUNDLING     Leaders / Fillers / Killers + Good/Better/Best
 4. HOW YOU CHARGE > WHAT        The monetization MODEL matters more than the number
 5. PRICING STRATEGY             Maximization / Penetration / Skimming
 6. LIVING BUSINESS CASE         Link Price–Value–Volume–Cost. Update every milestone.
 7. VALUE COMMUNICATION          Sell BENEFITS, not features
 8. BEHAVIORAL PRICING           Compromise / anchoring / signals / razor-blade / etc.
 9. MAINTAIN PRICE INTEGRITY     Try 3 nonprice actions BEFORE cutting price
```

## LEADERS / FILLERS / KILLERS — THE FEATURE TRIAGE

```
LEADER  — Drives the buying decision. High WTP.        → Always include.
FILLER  — Nice-to-have. Low individual WTP.            → Use to round out tiers.
KILLER  — Blows the deal if forced into the bundle.    → Eliminate or sell à la carte.
```

**Killer test:** valued by <20% of customers AND not valued at all by >20%. Killers are segment-dependent — heated seats are a Leader in Norway and a Killer in Singapore.

## GOOD / BETTER / BEST — DISTRIBUTION + FENCES

Healthy distribution:

```
≤25%   GOOD         ~70%   BETTER + BEST         ≥10%   BEST
```

Diagnostic readings:

```
>50% picking GOOD       → Trip-wire. Cut features from Good.
<10% picking BEST       → Premium tier is underpowered.
~70% in middle tiers    → Healthy.
```

**Fences are mandatory.** Every tier needs visible, defensible differences. Without fences, Good/Better/Best cannibalizes itself.

**Fence test:** *In 10 seconds, can a customer see what's missing from Good?* If no, your fence is broken.

## THE 5 MONETIZATION MODELS

```
SUBSCRIPTION             Continual usage                   Netflix, Adobe
DYNAMIC PRICING          Volatile demand / constrained     Uber, airlines
                         supply
AUCTIONS                 Seller's market, constrained      Google AdWords, eBay
                         inventory
PAY-AS-YOU-GO / ALT      Usage tracks value                Michelin per-mile,
METRIC                                                     GE engines per flight-hr
FREEMIUM                 Near-zero marginal AND fixed       LinkedIn, Dropbox
                         cost per user
```

**Freemium warning:** fails for ~90% of companies. Software conversion typically <10%. Games lose ~75% of users on day 1. Use only when fixed-AND-marginal cost per free user is near zero.

Models are **mix-and-matchable.** Costco = subscription + per-unit. OpenTable = subscription + per-transaction.

## THE 6 BEHAVIORAL PRICING TACTICS

```
1. COMPROMISE EFFECT       Always have 3 tiers. People avoid extremes.

2. ANCHORING               The Economist test: $59 digital vs $125 print vs
                           $125 print+digital made the bundle pick rate jump
                           32% → 84% by adding a useless decoy.

3. PRICE SIGNALS QUALITY   Ariely placebo study: a $2.50 pill produced 85%
                           pain relief; the same pill at $0.10 produced 61%.

4. RAZOR / RAZOR BLADES    Low upfront + ongoing supplies is preferred even
                           when total cost is identical.

5. PENNIES-A-DAY           $9.99/mo converts very differently from $120/yr.
                           Frame the price in the smallest credible unit.

6. PSYCHOLOGICAL           $69.99 works. $71 doesn't (drops acceptance >20%).
   THRESHOLDS              Stay below the threshold buyers anchor on.
```

**Caveat:** Behavioral tactics layer on top of rational value-based pricing, not instead of it.

## DECISION TREE — IS MY PRODUCT LIKELY TO FAIL?

```
Can I state the customer benefit in ONE sentence (not a feature list)?
├─ NO  → Likely FEATURE SHOCK
└─ YES → Has WTP been validated with real customers?
         ├─ NO  → Likely UNDEAD or MINIVATION
         └─ YES → Did the C-suite engage personally with pricing?
                  ├─ NO  → Likely HIDDEN GEM (won't get launched right)
                  └─ YES → On the right track
```

## DECISION TREE — WHICH MONETIZATION MODEL?

```
Is value tied to USAGE?
├─ YES → Alternative Metric (Michelin-style)
└─ NO  → Demand volatile or supply constrained?
        ├─ YES → Dynamic Pricing
        └─ NO  → Production cost near zero per user?
                ├─ YES → Freemium (ONLY if free users are near-zero-cost)
                └─ NO  → Subscription or per-unit
```

## DECISION TREE — SHOULD I CUT THE PRICE?

```
Sales below plan?
└─ YES → Have I identified the ROOT CAUSE?
         ├─ NO  → Diagnose first (the cause is usually NOT price)
         └─ YES → Is it actually pricing-specific?
                  ├─ NO  → Fix the actual problem
                  └─ YES → Have I tried 3 NONPRICE actions first?
                           (advertise more / add value / upgrade messaging)
                           ├─ NO  → Try those first
                           └─ YES → War-game the competitor reaction
                                    └─ Will I be worse off after they counter?
                                       → DON'T cut price.
```

## THE "BECAUSE" TEST

Every pricing decision must end with a "because" traceable to customer data.

```
Bad:   "We priced at $99 to be competitive."
Good:  "We priced at $99 BECAUSE 60% of segment B told us $100 was the
        threshold above which they'd reconsider, AND our value advantage
        justifies the high end of the range."
```

If you can't say "because customers told us X" — you don't have a pricing strategy, you have a guess.

## WTP RESEARCH CAVEAT

**Stated WTP and revealed WTP differ.** Customers predict their own behavior poorly in interviews. Treat WTP findings as a strong prior, not certainty.

Validate with:
- Paid pilots
- Pre-orders
- Live A/B price tests
- Money-back guarantees that convert reluctance into a binding commitment

## QUICK CHECKLIST — BEFORE DESIGNING A NEW PRODUCT

```
[ ] WTP conversations held with real customers
[ ] Segmented by NEEDS / VALUE / WTP — not demographics
[ ] Leaders, fillers, killers identified
[ ] Good/Better/Best configured WITH FENCES
[ ] Monetization model chosen and aligns with value
[ ] Pricing strategy documented (max / penetration / skimming)
[ ] Living business case links Price / Value / Volume / Cost
[ ] Benefit-not-feature messaging tested
[ ] Behavioral tactics considered
[ ] Team prepared to MAINTAIN price integrity post-launch
```

## WHEN NOT TO USE THIS FRAMEWORK

```
Pure cost-plus regulated environment    → Utilities, defense — price is set elsewhere
Pure commodity                          → Sugar, copper — price is the spot market
Very early stage with no concept yet    → Use Mom Test first
B2C impulse under $50                   → Simpler heuristics work
Geographic markets with no purchasing
power for your category                 → No amount of pricing wisdom fixes that
```

## INTEGRATION WITH OTHER FRAMEWORKS

- **The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick)** — Use Mom Test interviewing technique inside WTP conversations. Asking "would you pay $X?" produces lies; asking about past pricing behavior produces signal.
- **$100M Offers (Hormozi)** — Hormozi's Value Equation is the operational version of "design around the price." Use the Value Equation to maximize numerator/denominator; use this book to choose model and tier structure.
- **SPIN Selling (Rackham)** — In B2B, your price discovery happens during Implication and Need-payoff questions. The WTP "ceiling" surfaces when the customer articulates the cost of inaction.
