# BOOK 59: THE FOUR STEPS TO THE EPIPHANY
# Steve Blank
# ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## THE ONE LINE
Startups don't fail because the product doesn't work — they fail because the company never finds customers. Treat customer development as a parallel discipline to product development, not an afterthought.

## THE CORE INSIGHT

```
THE WRONG MODEL (what most startups do)

  Concept ──→ Product Dev ──→ Alpha / Beta ──→ LAUNCH
                                                  ↑
                                          "Where are the
                                           customers?"

THE RIGHT MODEL — CUSTOMER DEVELOPMENT (in parallel)

  ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐
  │ Customer │◀─▶│ Customer │◀─▶│ Customer │──▶│ Company  │
  │ Discovery│   │Validation│   │ Creation │   │ Building │
  └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘
       ↻               ↻               ↻
    iterate         iterate         iterate
```

Customer Development is **iterative**. Going back a step is not failure — it is the method.

> *"In a startup, no facts exist inside the building, only opinions."* — Blank

## THE 10 FLAWS OF THE PRODUCT-FIRST MODEL

```
 1. WHERE ARE THE CUSTOMERS?           Greatest risk is customer/market, not product
 2. FOCUS ON FIRST CUSTOMER SHIP DATE  FCS means Engineering is done, not customers
 3. EXECUTION INSTEAD OF LEARNING      Sales/marketing hired for what they know
 4. NO MEANINGFUL S&M MILESTONES       "Hire, fire, repeat" replaces real metrics
 5. PRODUCT DEV CLOCK MEASURES SALES   Clock to tell temperature — wrong measure
 6. PRODUCT DEV CLOCK MEASURES MKTG    Plans built in "a vacuum of real feedback"
 7. PREMATURE SCALING                  Hiring sales before someone wants to buy
 8. DEATH SPIRAL                       Burn → miss → fire VP Sales → fire VP Mktg → fire CEO
 9. NOT ALL STARTUPS ARE ALIKE         Four Market Types need different strategies
10. UNREALISTIC EXPECTATIONS           "Build it and they will come" is a prayer
```

## THE 4 MARKET TYPES — EVERYTHING DEPENDS ON THIS

```
EXISTING MARKET
  Higher performance than what's there
  Competitors define the basis of competition
  Time to profit: 12–18 months
  Key risk: Cost of entry (Lanchester rules)

NEW MARKET
  Enables something customers couldn't do before
  Competitor is non-consumption
  Time to profit: 3–7 years
  Key risk: Market may never materialize

RESEGMENTED — LOW-COST
  "Good enough" at substantially lower price
  Incumbents who abandon low margins
  Time to profit: 18–36 months
  Key risk: Must be profitable at the low price

RESEGMENTED — NICHE
  Radical enough to change rules for a SUBSET
  Incumbents who defend the profitable core
  Time to profit: 18–36 months
  Key risk: Segmentation must be exactly right
```

### THE MARKET-TYPE DECISION TREE

```
Are there existing companies selling comparable products?
├─ NO  → NEW MARKET (no competitors but no existing customers either)
└─ YES → Are you competing on the same dimensions (features, performance)?
         ├─ YES → EXISTING MARKET
         └─ NO  → Are you targeting a subset of customers?
                  ├─ YES, at lower price → RESEGMENTED LOW-COST
                  └─ YES, for a specific need → RESEGMENTED NICHE
```

### THE HANDSPRING / PALM TEST

Same team, same CEO (Donna Dubinsky), same product category, three years apart.

```
PALM (1996) — NEW MARKET
  PDA category didn't exist. Had to teach customers what a PDA could do.
  If they'd run an "Existing Market" playbook → zero dollars.

HANDSPRING (1999) — EXISTING MARKET
  PDA was a billion-dollar category. Differentiate on expandability.
  Drove demand from competitors. $170M revenue in 12 months.
```

**Identical product, identical team — wrong Market Type strategy = death.**

## STEP 1: CUSTOMER DISCOVERY

> *"Finding out who the customers for your product are and whether the problem you believe you are solving is important to them."*

### WHAT YOU ARE NOT DOING
- Collecting feature lists from prospects
- Running focus groups to design the product
- Writing a Marketing Requirements Document
- Trying to understand the needs of ALL customers

### WHAT YOU ARE DOING
- Testing whether the **founders' vision** matches a real, painful problem
- Building iteratively for the FEW, not the many
- Getting outside the building

> *"The initial product specification comes from the founders' vision, not the sum of a set of focus groups."*

### EARLYVANGELISTS — THE MOST IMPORTANT CUSTOMERS YOU'LL EVER FIND

Not all early customers are equal. Earlyvangelists have all five traits:

```
 1. HAS A PROBLEM                         Not latent — they know it exists
 2. UNDERSTANDS THE PROBLEM               Can articulate it and its cost
 3. ACTIVELY SEARCHING FOR A SOLUTION     Has a timetable
 4. HAS COBBLED TOGETHER AN INTERIM FIX   Proves the pain is real
 5. HAS OR CAN ACQUIRE BUDGET             Can actually buy
```

### THE CUSTOMER-PAIN HIERARCHY

```
"What problem?"                              → Latent need. Useless for 2 years.
"Yes, terrible. I work around it."           → Active but only papering symptoms.
"Losing $500K/yr. Need a fix by Q3."         → Visualized solution. Getting warm.
"Wrote a req to IT, they cobbled a tool      → HAS A HOMEMADE FIX. YES.
 but it keeps crashing."
"Budgeted $500K for a vendor."               → HAS BUDGET. THE ULTIMATE CUSTOMER.
```

### THE 6 HYPOTHESES (write these down BEFORE leaving the building)

```
1. Customer & Problem hypothesis
2. Product hypothesis
3. Distribution & Pricing hypothesis
4. Demand Creation hypothesis
5. Market Type hypothesis
6. Competitive hypothesis
```

### PASS / FAIL

Pass: founders' vision matches a real, painful, paid-for problem. → Step 2.
Fail: iterate within Discovery, or exit.

## STEP 2: CUSTOMER VALIDATION

> *"Customer Validation is where the rubber meets the road."*

### THE GOAL
Build a **repeatable sales roadmap** — "the playbook of the proven and repeatable sales process that has been field-tested by successfully selling the product to early customers."

### WHAT YOU ARE NOT DOING
- Staffing a sales team
- Executing a "sales strategy"
- Generating revenue as the goal (revenue is a side effect)

> *"Building a roadmap to sales success, rather than building a sales organization, is the heart of Customer Validation."*

### THE TEST FOR EXITING VALIDATION

Can you describe — on a single page — exactly:
- Who buys (title, company size, industry)?
- Why they buy (the pain, the moment they reach for a solution)?
- How they buy (the steps, who else is involved, the timeline)?
- What they pay (and how the pricing was validated)?

If "yes" → proceed to Customer Creation. If "no" → iterate, return to Discovery if needed.

## STEP 3: CUSTOMER CREATION

> *"Driving demand into the company's sales channel — matched to the Market Type."*

This is where most startups blow up. They run an Existing-Market playbook (paid acquisition, comparison campaigns, beat-the-competitor messaging) in a New Market — and burn millions teaching nobody.

```
EXISTING MARKET  → Position against competitors. Beat them on a known dimension.
NEW MARKET       → Educate. Build the category. Find the early adopters first.
RESEGMENTED      → Position against the incumbents' weakness; not on their axis.
```

**The cardinal sin:** Spending on demand-creation BEFORE the sales roadmap from Step 2 is proven. You will scale a broken process.

## STEP 4: COMPANY BUILDING

> *"Transitioning from a learning-and-discovery organization to an execution-and-growth organization."*

The transition the company has to make:

```
LEARNING ORG (Steps 1–3)         EXECUTION ORG (Step 4)
──────────────────────────       ──────────────────────────
Founders run discovery           Functional leaders run departments
Everyone does everything         Specialization and roles
Failure = learning               Failure = miss
Hypothesis → test → iterate      Plan → execute → measure
"Outside the building" mindset   Operating cadence, KPIs, processes
```

Most startups die here too — by transitioning **too early** (before the roadmap is real) or **too late** (chaos persists past product-market fit).

## CUSTOMER DEVELOPMENT VS. PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT

They are not substitutes — they run in parallel.

```
Product Development asks:    "Can we build it?"
Customer Development asks:   "Will anyone buy it?"

Both questions must be answered YES before scaling.
Failing on either kills the company.
```

## WHEN NOT TO USE THIS FRAMEWORK

```
You've already crossed the chasm        → Use Crossing the Chasm instead
You're optimizing an existing funnel    → This is for search, not execution
You're product-managing in a large org  → Steve Blank's later work fits better
Service business with proven model      → You already have a roadmap
```

## INTEGRATION WITH OTHER FRAMEWORKS

- **The Lean Startup (Ries)** built directly on this. "Build-Measure-Learn" is the productized version of Customer Development. Use Blank for the strategic frame, Ries for the operating loop.
- **The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick)** gives you the question technique for Step 1. Discovery without Mom-Test discipline produces false positives.
- **Crossing the Chasm (Moore)** picks up where this leaves off: after Customer Validation, mainstream-market dynamics take over.
