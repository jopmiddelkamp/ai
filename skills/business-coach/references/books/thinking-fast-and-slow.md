## THE ONE LINE
Your brain has two systems — fast intuitive thinking and slow deliberate thinking — and understanding which to use when is the key to better decisions.

## THE TWO SYSTEMS

```
SYSTEM 1 (Fast, Automatic):
→ Runs automatically, no effort
→ Uses pattern recognition and heuristics
→ Makes most decisions (95%+ of your choices)
→ Often right on familiar problems
→ Systematically wrong on unfamiliar or complex problems

SYSTEM 2 (Slow, Deliberate):
→ Requires conscious effort
→ Analytical, logical
→ Accurate on complex problems
→ Lazy — avoids engaging when System 1 has an answer
→ Can be wrong when tired, rushed, or emotional

THE PROBLEM:
System 1 gives an answer. System 2 accepts it.
We think we're using System 2 when we're actually rationalizing System 1's answer.
```

## THE COGNITIVE BIASES — BUSINESS CRITICAL

```
ANCHORING:
The first number you hear disproportionately influences your judgment.
EXAMPLE: "Our normal price is $10,000, but for you today $7,500"
→ $7,500 feels like a deal even if the fair price is $4,000.

BUSINESS USE: Always anchor high in negotiations.
BUSINESS PROTECTION: Research independent fair value before negotiating.

AVAILABILITY HEURISTIC:
We judge probability by how easily examples come to mind.
EXAMPLE: After hearing about a plane crash: fear of flying spikes.
EXAMPLE: Your friend who succeeded with that strategy = you overestimate its success rate.

BUSINESS USE: Use data, not memorable anecdotes, for strategy decisions.

SUNK COST FALLACY:
Continuing a course of action because of past investment.
EXAMPLE: Keeping a failing employee because you've invested so much in training.
EXAMPLE: Continuing a product line because you spent $1M developing it.

RULE: Past costs are irrelevant to future decisions. Only future value matters.
"What would we do if we were deciding today with no history?"

LOSS AVERSION:
Losses feel ~2x more painful than equivalent gains feel good.
EXAMPLE: Losing $100 feels worse than gaining $100 feels good.

BUSINESS IMPLICATIONS:
→ Customers hate losing features more than they like gaining features
→ Price increases feel worse than discounts feel good
→ Losing a client feels worse than gaining an equivalent one

OVERCONFIDENCE:
People consistently overestimate their abilities.
93% of drivers believe they're above average.
74% of fund managers believe they'll outperform the market.

BUSINESS IMPLICATION:
Your business forecasts are almost certainly too optimistic.
Build in 30% buffer on all positive projections.
Take risk scenarios seriously.

THE PLANNING FALLACY:
Projects take longer and cost more than planned.
ALWAYS. It's not a surprise. It's a law.

RULE: Whatever you estimate: Double the time and 1.5x the cost.
Then you're usually approximately right.
```

## THE PREMORTEM (Kahneman's Technique)

```
BEFORE a project, decision, or launch:
"It's 12 months from now. This failed completely. 
What happened?"

WHY IT WORKS:
Shifts from "what could go wrong" (hard to imagine)
to "this already went wrong, what was the cause" (easier)

Activates System 2 on the decision.
Surfaces risks that overconfidence hides.
Creates contingency thinking before you need it.

RESULTS:
Teams who do premortems: More realistic plans, better contingencies
Teams who don't: More surprised by "unexpected" failures
```
