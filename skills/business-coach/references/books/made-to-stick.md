## THE ONE LINE
The enemy of every message is the **Curse of Knowledge** — once you know something, you can't imagine not knowing it. The SUCCESs framework is the systematic antidote.

## THE CORE INSIGHT — THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE

The tappers-and-listeners experiment: tap a familiar song on a table; listeners identify it correctly only **2.5%** of the time, while the tappers predict **50%**. The tapper hears the melody in their head; the listener hears taps. Every founder, expert, and leader is a tapper.

```
WHAT COMMUNICATORS DO                  WHAT STICKY MESSAGES DO
──────────────────────────             ──────────────────────────────────
Bury the lead in context               Lead with the core (SIMPLE)
Start with what's expected             Start with what's surprising (UNEXPECTED)
Speak in abstractions                  Speak in sensory, tangible terms (CONCRETE)
Rely on their own authority            Let the message prove itself (CREDIBLE)
Appeal to logic and reason             Appeal to identity and feeling (EMOTIONAL)
Present conclusions                    Tell stories that simulate action (STORIES)
```

## THE SUCCESs FRAMEWORK

Six principles. Not a checklist — a diagnostic toolkit. Most failing messages break on 1–2 specific principles. Find which ones and fix those.

```
S  IMPLE       Find the core. Share the core. Nothing else.
U  NEXPECTED   Surprise to get attention. Curiosity to keep it.
C  ONCRETE     Sensory, tangible, specific. No abstractions.
C  REDIBLE     Make them believe — or better, let them prove it themselves.
E  MOTIONAL    Make them care. Belief without feeling does not produce action.
S  TORIES      Flight simulators for the brain. People rehearse, then act.
```

(The lowercase "s" is the authors' own joke.)

## 1. SIMPLE — FIND THE CORE

This is not dumbing down. It is forced prioritization: the single most important thing, said in a way that is compact AND profound.

**Commander's Intent.** The military's solution to the Curse of Knowledge. Detailed battle plans fall apart on contact with the enemy. So officers communicate the one thing that matters: *"Break the will of the enemy in this region."* Soldiers improvise around that intent when the plan fails.

**Your Commander's Intent:** If your audience remembers ONLY ONE THING, what must it be?

### Tools for simplicity

```
INVERTED PYRAMID     Lead with the most important info first. Journalism standard.
GENERATIVE ANALOGY   Compress an idea into a schema people already have.
                     Disney calls employees "cast members" — generates correct
                     behavior across thousands of situations.
HIGH-CONCEPT PITCH   Describe via known reference points.
                     "Jaws on a spaceship" (Alien). "Die Hard on a bus" (Speed).
PROVERBS             Compact AND profound. "A bird in the hand is worth two
                     in the bush."
```

**The accuracy trap:** Do not sacrifice memorability for completeness. *"Maximize shareholder value"* is technically accurate and generates no useful behavior. Southwest's *"THE low-fare airline"* is less precise and drives every operating decision in the company.

**Test:** Can a front-line employee use your message to make a decision you'd agree with — without asking you?

## 2. UNEXPECTED — SURPRISE + CURIOSITY

```
GETTING ATTENTION   SURPRISE     Break a schema (violate expectations)
KEEPING ATTENTION   CURIOSITY    Open a knowledge gap, then fill it
```

**The surprise sequence:**
1. Identify the central message.
2. Figure out what is counterintuitive about it.
3. Communicate it in a way that breaks the audience's guessing machine.

**Gap Theory of Curiosity (Loewenstein, 1994):** Curiosity is the gap between what we know and what we want to know. To make people curious: (1) point out what they DON'T know, (2) highlight that they should care, (3) fill the gap.

**Mysteries vs. surprise:** Surprise gets attention in a moment. Mystery keeps attention across time. Structure complex messages as mysteries when possible.

**Warning:** Surprise for its own sake (gimmicks) is counterproductive. The surprise must serve the core.

## 3. CONCRETE — SENSORY LANGUAGE

The easiest of the six to embrace, and possibly the most effective.

**Why we slip into abstraction:** Expertise makes you think in patterns and principles. You forget the audience thinks in concrete examples. Engineers redraw the schematic when they should walk to the factory floor.

**Velcro Theory of Memory:** The more sensory hooks an idea has (sights, sounds, smells, touch), the more it sticks. Abstract ideas have few hooks. Concrete ideas have many.

```
ABSTRACT                              CONCRETE
─────────────────────────────         ──────────────────────────────────────────
"Increase customer satisfaction"      "When a guest's towel falls in the pool,
                                       replace it before they ask."
"The next great search engine"        "Seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop Miami →
                                       NYC, land on Runway 4-22 at La Guardia."
"We value diversity"                  "Our longest-tenured member is a 73-year-old
                                       retired government worker with no prior
                                       dance experience."
"Improve employee productivity"       "If wireless saves an employee 1–2 minutes
                                       per day, it's paid for itself."
```

**The "white things" test:** Name white things in the world (hard). Name white things in your refrigerator (easy). Concreteness focuses and mobilizes thinking.

**For founders:** Visit actual customers. General Mills sent the Hamburger Helper team into customers' homes ("Fingertips" program). Abstract data produced abstract strategy. Concrete observation produced an 11% sales increase.

## 4. CREDIBLE — LET THE MESSAGE PROVE ITSELF

Three sources of credibility, from external to internal:

```
AUTHORITIES (external)           Experts with credentials, celebrities — AND
                                 anti-authorities (real people with lived
                                 experience). Pam Laffin, a 29-year-old smoker
                                 dying of emphysema, was more persuasive against
                                 smoking than any Surgeon General.

THE MESSAGE ITSELF (internal)    Vivid details. Human-scale statistics. The
                                 Sinatra Test. One example that single-handedly
                                 proves credibility for a domain.

THE AUDIENCE (self-verify)       Testable credentials. "See for yourself."
                                 "Are you better off than you were four years
                                 ago?" — Reagan.
                                 "Where's the beef?" — Wendy's.
```

**Statistics rule:** Never use a number in isolation. Always use statistics to illustrate a RELATIONSHIP. *"5,000 nuclear warheads"* means nothing. One BB dropped in a steel bucket (Hiroshima), then 5,000 BBs poured in (current arsenal) — the roar that went on and on, followed by dead silence. That's a statistic that changes minds.

**Human-scale principle:** Reframe statistics in terms people can intuit. Not "sun-to-earth accuracy" but "New York to LA, within two-thirds of an inch."

## 5. EMOTIONAL — MAKE THEM CARE

> *"If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will."* — Mother Teresa

**The Rokia study:** People donated $2.38 to save one named girl (Rokia, age 7, from Mali). People donated $1.14 when shown statistics about millions suffering. People given BOTH the statistics AND Rokia donated $1.43. **Statistics actively suppressed emotional giving.** The analytical frame of mind is the enemy of empathy.

### Three strategies for making people care

```
ASSOCIATION         Link your idea to something they already care about.
                    "Don't Mess with Texas" linked littering to Texan PRIDE,
                    not to environmental duty. Worked because of identity.

SELF-INTEREST       Answer "What's in it for you?" — but go beyond features
                    to identity. Maslow's peak (esteem, self-actualization),
                    not Maslow's basement (save time, save money).

IDENTITY            Appeal to who people ARE or want to be.
                    "What would someone like me do?" (James March's identity
                    model of decision-making.)
```

**Maslow's Basement problem:** Marketers default to base-level appeals (save money, save time). The audience often cares more about self-actualization, belonging, or esteem. Aim higher.

**Semantic-stretch warning:** Some words have lost emotional power through overuse — *"unique," "quality," "innovative," "cutting-edge."* If a word could describe any company in your industry, it is emotionally dead.

## 6. STORIES — FLIGHT SIMULATORS FOR THE BRAIN

Mental-simulation research: imagining an event activates the same brain regions as experiencing it. Stories don't just inform — they prepare people to act.

### Three inspirational plot types

```
CHALLENGE        Underdog overcomes obstacles.
                 Use when you need to inspire persistence, effort, courage.

CONNECTION       People bridge a divide (racial, class, demographic).
                 Use when you need to build empathy, teamwork, tolerance.

CREATIVITY       Someone has an insight that solves a puzzle.
                 Use when you need to inspire innovation, new thinking.
```

**Springboard stories.** Stephen Denning at the World Bank could not convince leadership to embrace knowledge management with data and arguments. He told one story: a health worker in Zambia logging onto the CDC website at 2 a.m. to solve a malaria problem. That single story launched an institution-wide transformation.

**Spotting vs. creating:** You don't need to invent stories. The best ones already exist in your company, among your customers, in your data. Your job is to spot them and retell them.

**The speakers-vs-stickers experiment:** After a round of one-minute speeches mixing statistics and stories: only **5%** of listeners remembered a statistic. **63%** remembered a story. Stories are ~12× more memorable than statistics.

## THE SUCCESs DIAGNOSTIC

When a message isn't working, find the broken principle:

```
"It's too complicated"            → SIMPLE       Find Commander's Intent. Cut.
"People tune out immediately"     → UNEXPECTED   Lead with counterintuitive. Open a gap.
"They nod but forget by tomorrow" → CONCRETE     Replace abstractions with sensory detail.
"They don't believe us"           → CREDIBLE     Anti-authorities, Sinatra Test, "see for yourself."
"They believe but don't care"     → EMOTIONAL    Focus on the one, not the mass. Identity.
"They care but don't act"         → STORIES      Give a mental simulation. Springboard story.
```

## THE COMMUNICATION FRAMEWORK

```
I need my audience to...         Use this principle:
PAY ATTENTION                    Unexpected (surprise + curiosity gap)
UNDERSTAND AND REMEMBER          Concrete (sensory, tangible, specific)
BELIEVE / AGREE                  Credible (authorities, details, Sinatra, testable)
CARE                             Emotional (individual focus, identity, self-interest)
ACT                              Stories (mental simulation, springboard)
```

Simple isn't listed because it is the prerequisite for all of them. If you haven't found the core, none of the other five can save you.

## WHEN NOT TO USE THIS FRAMEWORK

```
Channel / distribution problem      → Use Traction (right channel, right audience)
Brand-narrative structure           → Use StoryBrand (your customer as hero)
Category positioning                → Use Obviously Awesome (where you compete)
Internal data / dashboarding        → SUCCESs is for messages, not metrics
```

## INTEGRATION WITH OTHER FRAMEWORKS

- **StoryBrand (Miller)** structures a brand's narrative arc. Made to Stick provides the quality control inside each beat of the narrative. Use both: StoryBrand for the skeleton, Made to Stick for the muscle.
- **Traction (Weinberg & Mares)** tells you WHERE to deliver the message. Made to Stick tells you HOW to make the message land when it arrives.
- **The Challenger Sale** uses a reframe to teach the customer something true and counterintuitive — that is the UNEXPECTED principle applied to B2B sales.

## CLAUDE PROMPT

```
Run my message through the SUCCESs framework.

The message: [paste exactly as I'd say it]
The audience: [describe]
The action I want them to take: [specific]

Run the diagnostic:
1. SIMPLE — what is my Commander's Intent? Is it actually findable in the
   current message?
2. UNEXPECTED — what surprises the audience? Is there a curiosity gap?
3. CONCRETE — list every abstraction in the message and rewrite one
   sensory replacement for each.
4. CREDIBLE — what's the Sinatra-Test example? Any testable credential?
5. EMOTIONAL — am I focusing on one specific person? Am I appealing to
   identity or just self-interest?
6. STORIES — is there a springboard story I can substitute for a key
   argument?

Then tell me which 1–2 principles I'm breaking hardest and give me
a rewritten version that fixes them.
```
